HTML is how documents are born now

An increasing share of the world's documents start life as HTML: invoices from web apps, reports from dashboards, certificates from generators, emails, receipts, tickets, generated statements. HTML is fluid — it reflows to any screen — which is its genius and its problem. When a document must be filed, printed, archived or sent somewhere that demands fixed pages, fluid stops being a feature. PagePress freezes HTML into PDF the way a printing press freezes type: faithfully, paginated, at a standard size.

The workflow is honest about its inputs. You paste the HTML — from a template, a generator, a 'view source', an export — and a live preview shows the render immediately, in the tool, before any conversion. What you see is genuinely what gets pressed, because the same rendered page is what the converter slices into PDF pages. No surprises of the 'the PDF came back different' genus, because there is only one render in the whole pipeline.

How pressing works: render, then paginate

PagePress renders your HTML in a real browser context at double scale, so text and images are captured crisply, then slices the tall rendered canvas into page-height sections and lays each section onto its own PDF page. This render-and-slice method handles arbitrary HTML — flexbox, tables, inline SVG-free styling, web-safe fonts — with the fidelity of the browser itself, because it is the browser itself doing the work. External images load if their servers allow cross-origin canvas use; self-contained HTML with inline styles always presses perfectly.

One honest behaviour follows from slicing: a page break can land mid-table or mid-paragraph if your content's height demands it, exactly as a tall screenshot would. Authors who control the HTML can steer breaks with CSS — page-break-inside: avoid on blocks — and the live preview plus a glance at the output's page count tells you where the cuts fell. For reports, invoices and certificates — the dominant jobs — the method is fast, faithful and entirely sufficient.

How to convert HTML to PDF, step by step

Paste your HTML into the editor; the preview updates as you type, so fix any obvious issue at the source. Choose page size and orientation to match the content's intent — portrait for documents, landscape for wide tables and dashboards. Press the button and the tool renders, slices and assembles: a handful of pages takes seconds, all on your device.

Download and inspect the joins: scroll the PDF and check the page boundaries, especially through tables and images. If a break amputates something important, return to the HTML and add breathing room or a break-avoid rule at that spot, then press again — iteration is free and local. Save the PDF with a meaningful name; the HTML stays in the editor if a revised edition is needed later in the session.

Why paste-only is a principle, not a limitation

Visitors reasonably ask why the tool will not simply fetch a URL. The honest answer is architectural: browsers forbid a page from fetching and re-rendering arbitrary other sites — the same-origin rules that keep the web's security model intact. Tools that claim to convert 'any URL' route the address through their servers, fetching your page with their machine, seeing everything it contains, including the intranet links and token-bearing URLs users inevitably paste.

PagePress refuses that bargain. Paste-only keeps the pipeline local: your HTML — which may be an internal report template, an unreleased design, a statement layout with real data — is rendered and pressed entirely within your browser tab. Nothing is fetched, proxied or retained. The thirty seconds it takes to copy source HTML is the entire cost, and for documents with anything sensitive in them, it is a cost worth paying every time.

Privacy: your markup never leaves the tab

HTML pasted into PagePress often contains the very data a document exists to carry — names, figures, internal wording. The tool renders it in a sandboxed preview inside your browser and performs the entire conversion with libraries on your device. No server receives the markup, the render or the PDF; closing the tab erases all of it from memory.

Local pressing also means no size tiers and no queues: a long annual-report page presses as happily as a one-page invoice. When the material is confidential — and report templates usually are, right up until publication — a converter that never sees your content is not a luxury. It is simply the correct tool shape.

Preparing HTML that presses beautifully

Self-contained HTML presses best: inline styles travel with the document, while external stylesheets must load before rendering, so paste complete markup whenever you can. Give the content a print-minded width in your CSS — a max-width container keeps lines at a readable measure on wide pages. Anticipate the slice: if a table or card must not split, mark it with page-break-inside: avoid, and test with a deliberately long document once to learn where your template's natural breaks fall. Use real image files with cross-origin permission or inline them as data URIs to guarantee they appear. And keep a copy of the source HTML with the PDF it produced; the pair is your reproducible press, ready to strike a corrected edition in under a minute.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can PagePress convert a web page from its URL?

No — deliberately. Browsers block cross-site fetching, and URL-converting services must fetch the page on their servers, seeing its full contents. Paste the page's HTML here instead and everything stays on your device.

Why did a table split across two pages?

The render is sliced at exact page height. Add CSS such as page-break-inside: avoid to key blocks in your HTML, or adjust spacing so the break lands between sections, then press again.

Do external images appear in the PDF?

Images that permit cross-origin use render normally. If an image is missing, its server blocked canvas access — inline the image as a data URI in your HTML for a guaranteed result.

Which page sizes and orientations are supported?

A4 and US Letter, each in portrait or landscape — covering the overwhelming majority of document, report and handout work.

Is the output text selectable?

Pages are faithful high-resolution renders of your HTML, so text is not selectable in the PDF. For a text-based document, build it as text (PlainPDF) instead; PagePress is for rendered layouts.

Is my HTML uploaded anywhere?

Never. Preview, rendering and PDF assembly all run in your browser tab. Nothing is transmitted or stored.
